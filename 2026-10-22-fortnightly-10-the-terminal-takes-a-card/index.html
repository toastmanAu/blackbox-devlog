Fortnightly #10: the terminal learns to take a card

2026-10-22 · wave-2firmwarehardware

Three ways to pay now, and the newest one is the first that doesn’t need the chain at the moment of sale. CKB and Bitcoin both put the network in the loop when the customer taps. A prepaid card takes it out: the money is on the card, the debit happens on the card, and the terminal reconciles with the chain afterwards, in its own time.

A card that carries its own balance

The card holds a balance, a counter and a MAC. A tap reads the record, decides whether the sale is allowed, writes the new balance and counter back, and signs the result — all card-side, with no server and no transaction. The terminal holds a debit key that can spend down a card but cannot create value: issuing and loading happen in the Vault, which owns the on-chain reserve behind each card. The posture is the one this project keeps for every rail — the terminal holds no key that can mint money.

Settlement is a separate, later act. The terminal keeps a journal of every completed tap and, when it can reach the Vault, sends the batch; the Vault verifies each entry, spends the matching reserve cell, and records the result on chain. Until then the terminal runs on an unsettled cap, so the worst case of a terminal that never phones home is bounded rather than open-ended.

This is a demo tier. It runs on cheap NTAG cards protected by our own MAC rather than the DESFire EV3 secure element the shipping card will use, behind a compile-time flag, on testnet. It exists so the whole flow — issue, tap, debit, refund, settle, revoke — is real and exercised long before the secure silicon and its keys arrive.

Getting a tap wrong, safely

The part worth the month is what happens when a tap doesn’t go cleanly, because that is where money is lost or duplicated.

Every debit is written to the journal as a write-ahead intent before the card is touched, and removed only once the card write is confirmed and the entry durably stored. So a card pulled out mid-write, a brown-out between the card and the journal, a reader glitch — each lands in a state the terminal can name. A write that is known not to have landed charges nothing. A write whose outcome is unknown parks, and the next tap of that same card resolves it to exactly one charge or none — never two. At the bench, cards were yanked mid-write on purpose, five times, and the ledger came out with exactly five charges and no doubles and no losses.

A sale cancelled at the instant its card was being charged is the nastiest case: the money moved but the sale is gone. That now raises a flag the cashier must acknowledge, and — a fix from the pre-merge security review — the same card is refused a second tap until they do, so an orphaned charge is handed back by refund rather than silently doubled. The guard is held until the operator clears it, not on a timer, because a timer is exactly long enough for an unhurried cashier to charge twice.

Vouchers, and a key you can hold

Two companions to the card, both on the Vault side.

Item vouchers put a product rather than a balance on a tag — “1 × coffee” — each backed by a minimum-size chain cell and settled through the same endpoint as a card tap; a used-up voucher closes its cell automatically once the settlement lands. Staff keys are the authorisation side: any genuine NXP NTAG21x, recognised by the originality signature NXP puts on its silicon and verified once with a hand-written, verify-only elliptic-curve check. A price override needs a staff tag. The cheap demo cards turned out to be counterfeit — they answer like the real part but cannot produce that signature — which breaks nothing for cards (our MAC protects those) and is precisely why staff keys lean on the signature instead.

The freeze

One bench session the confirmation screen began freezing, hard, every time a successful card debit was immediately followed by a settlement sync. The heartbeat task kept printing; the UI task sat dead in a five-millisecond sleep it never woke from.

The cause was not in the money code at all. An SPI-flash write on the ESP32-S3 briefly stops the other CPU core with its caches off. The task doing the journal and settlement writes was unpinned, so it ran on the core opposite the UI — and its flash write dropped the UI core’s clock tick, so the UI’s sleep never ended. The fix is one word per task: pin the flash writers to the same core the UI runs on, and the stall lands on the networking core, which is built to absorb it. Seventeen hours of uptime and the full bench run later, not a single freeze. No payment logic changed — only which core a task is allowed to run on. The same latent bug was found and pinned in the Bitcoin header path, where it had not surfaced yet.

Also this fortnight

Adding a product to inventory no longer means typing a thirteen-digit code on glass: there’s a Scan barcode button on the Add Item form that arms the head in place and drops the code into the field, keeping the name and price already typed. And the bench caught the Add Item keyboard’s space bar hiding under the footer bar — created too early in the draw order — now fixed.

Next

The prepaid card work has landed on the board-port branch; the remaining items are a watchdog that reboots a genuinely wedged terminal without false-firing mid-update, certificate pinning so settlement can’t be spoofed on a hostile LAN, and the on-POS half of vouchers and staff keys. And the secure-element card itself, once the DESFire stock and a genuine staff tag are on the bench.
